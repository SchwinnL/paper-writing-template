# General research proposal writing instructions

### The Narrative: Foundation of a Great Proposal

A proposal should present one specific core goal, which is connected to one to three specific concrete claims or objectives that you believe to be true and achievable. Achieving the core goal should be impactful and not incremental. Everything else exists to support this narrative. The second pillar is rigorous methodology and description on how to get evidence for how these claims will be investigated.

**What makes a good narrative:**
- Motivate why someone should care about these objectives
- Contextualize them in existing literature and current limitations
- Communicate the proposed methodology precisely with all relevant technical detail
- Provide sufficient preliminary evidence or robust justification to support the feasibility of the work (good to use own prior work for that where possible)

**Key questions for finding your narrative:**
- Which outcomes would be most exciting to show the review committee?
- What seems particularly important? Why should anyone care?
- What is hard about what you propose that perhaps no one else has done?

**On novelty and impact:**
- Be extremely clear about what is novel and how it is novel
- Liberally cite relevant papers and explain how your proposed work differs and advances the field
- Frame the impact clearly: what happens if the project succeeds, and what do we learn if it fails?

**Introduction / Motivation:**
- Paragraph 1: Context, what topic are we studying, what is the key motivating question, why does it matter?
- Paragraph 2: Technical background / Problem, what are the current limitations in this field?
- Paragraph 3: Key vision, what exactly is our main objective with key nuance, detail, and context?
- Paragraph 4: Impact, what should the reviewers take away, what are the implications, why is this a big deal?
- End with a bullet-point list of proposed contributions or objectives.

**State of the Art / Background:**
- Do not be too specific. Proposals answer high level questions and bigger goals than papers.

**Work Program / Methodology:**
- Explain what you actually plan to do in full detail, avoiding vague statements like "we will explore"
- Break down the work into concrete activities, experiments, analyses, and baselines
- Explain why your approach is reasonable and relevant to your objectives
- Specify technical choices, potential risks, and mitigation strategies (what if Plan A fails?)

**Impact / Conclusion:**
- Discuss broader implications, general takeaways, and how the research aligns with the funding agency's goals
- Reiterate why the team is uniquely positioned to execute this work

### Figures and Tables

**Captions:**
- Give context on what the figure shows, the nuance and intended interpretation, and key technical detail
- Ideally, the reader understands everything from just the figure and caption

### Writing Style and Clarity

**Core principles:**
- Be precise: Choose words carefully so you say what you mean; replace vague terms like "performance" with "accuracy" or "speed"
- Be concise: Most of us write in an overly wordy style; after writing initial text, try to delete around one-third of the words
- Avoid complex sentence structure: Research is already difficult; don't make it harder with run-on sentences
- Use consistent phrasing: Avoid synonyms for work-specific terminology at all costs
- Use simple language: Avoid rare words or sounding "fancy"; English is not the first language for many scientists
- Follow the writing style in the examples folder

**Active voice:**
- Passive voice is overused, clunky, and obscures who did what, avoid it
- Never use passive tense; always specify the actor ("We propose to...")

**Sentence structure:**
- Put the verb as early in the sentence as possible (early verbs make sentences easier to parse)
- If a sentence gets too long, split it into two: one sentence, one idea
- Don't use long sentences with a lot of actual content; split them
- Long sentences are fine if they have simple, easy-to-understand words

**Paragraphs:**
- Lead and end paragraphs with strong, clear sentences; middle sentences are for elaboration
- Make sure every sentence adds information
- Ask about every word/sentence: "Is this necessary?" and "Can I phrase this more simply?"

**Words and phrases to remove or replace:**
- Remove: actually, a bit, fortunately, "to our knowledge," "note that," "observe that," "try to," very/really/extremely
- Replace: want, hope, contractions ("it's" to "it is")
- Avoid words in quotation marks (a way to sneak imprecise or "dodgy" words in)
- Unfold apostrophes (X's Y to The Y of X)
- Limit hedging ("may" or "can"), hedge words should almost always be dropped

**Other style points:**
- No Em Dashes: Do not use em dashes. Use parentheses, commas, or split the sentence instead. Also avoid using ; symbols
- Minimize pronouns; if you must use "this," "those," etc., use them as adjectives (e.g., "this objective")
- Don't repeat similar-sounding words within a paragraph or sentence
- Don't start every sentence with "We", add just a bit of variation
- "On the other hand" shouldn't come without "On the one hand"
- Don't use comparatives without explicitly specifying what two things are being compared
- Avoid grandiose language or overly broad claims without preliminary evidence
- Avoid anthropomorphisms ("knowledge") of AI algorithms
- Avoid subjective claims, adjectives are usually red flags
- When shortening, avoid removing transitions. Rather focus on substituting words and rephrasing in ways that do not change the meaning of sentences. This includes searching for filler words.
